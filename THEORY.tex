\noindent A limit order book is a piece of exchange infrastructure: the thing
between ``an order arrives'' and ``a trade happens.'' You are building the fast
version. Each milestone below pairs \emph{what you build} with \emph{the theory
that explains why it's built that way} --- because in this domain, the theory
\emph{is} the implementation.

\subsection*{Theory: the wall of notes}

A farmer's market has a wall where buyers pin ``I'll buy at \$1.00, need 10''
and sellers pin ``selling at \$1.05, have 20.'' The \emph{book} is that wall,
kept sorted. The \emph{matching engine} is the clerk who rips notes off when
they match. Two rules govern everything:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Price priority} --- a new buyer trades with the
        \emph{cheapest} seller first. Best price wins.
  \item \textbf{Time priority} --- at the same price, earliest note trades
        first. FIFO queue per level.
\end{enumerate}

``Price-time priority'' is the entire spec. The only verbs are
\texttt{add}/\texttt{cancel}/\texttt{modify}; the only output is \texttt{Trade}
reports (always at the \emph{resting} order's price). A real exchange is this
loop, millions of times per second.

\subsection*{Theory: correctness is defined by behavior, not internals}

Your \texttt{Book} and \texttt{NaiveBook} share one contract. The tests don't
care which they run --- that's the point. Two consequences worth internalizing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{State machine view.} The book is a deterministic machine:
        events in, state transitions, trades out. Structurally identical to
        your emulator: \texttt{cycle()} became ``apply next event.''
  \item \textbf{Differential testing.} Same tape through two implementations
        must yield identical trades. ``Is it correct?'' becomes ``does it
        match the reference?'' --- falsifiable forever, for free.
\end{itemize}

Get it right first; every optimization afterward is continuously verified
against a known-good oracle.

\subsection*{Theory 1: memory is the cost, not compute}

The CPU is ~100x faster at arithmetic than at fetching from DRAM:

\begin{diagram}
executing one instruction on data in a register:   ~0.3 ns
L1 hit ~1 ns | L2 ~12 cy | L3 ~45 cy | DRAM ~200-300 cy (~100 ns)
\end{diagram}

The gap exists physically (DRAM is a separate chip, capacitors, distance) and
historically (CPU speed grew ~50\%/yr, DRAM latency ~7\%/yr --- the ``memory
wall''; caches are the workaround). \textbf{1 cache miss $\approx$ 300
instructions} --- so op-count alone is the wrong cost model.

\subsection*{Theory 2: dependent vs.\ independent misses}

Misses only serialize if they're \emph{dependent} --- when the next address is
hidden inside data you haven't fetched yet:

\begin{diagram}
independent (array):  known addresses -> fire all -> waits OVERLAP
dependent  (tree):    node -> next addr inside it -> serial, ~100ns each
\end{diagram}

The CPU keeps $\sim$10+ fetches in flight, but only for computable addresses.
Pointer chasing forbids overlap; the prefetcher can't help either.
\textbf{Case-2 structures (tree, list) are the worst case:} maximum dependent
misses, zero parallelism.

\subsection*{Theory 3: the address formula and its three conditions}

\begin{diagram}
f(i) = base + i * size     <- works only if:
  1. fixed-size elements   (constant stride; variable size => scan)
  2. stored INLINE         (vector<T*> is contiguous but still chases;
                            the object must BE at the address, not pointed)
  3. back-to-back          (contiguous; else no formula exists)
\end{diagram}

Bonus: cache fetches in \textbf{64-byte lines} --- contiguity means each miss
carries ~16 useful elements, not 1. Overlap fixes \emph{latency}; lines fix
\emph{fetch count}. You need both.

\subsection*{Theory 4: escaping the comparison model}

$\Omega(\log N)$ ordered access is a theorem \emph{only} for comparison-based
structures (``is $a < b$?'' gives 1 bit per step). Prices are bounded integers
--- an integer in a known range isn't just orderable, \emph{it's an address}:

\begin{diagram}
levels[price - LO]   <- the whole "search" is one subtraction
\end{diagram}

Same escape as radix sort beating $\Omega(N\log N)$. Honest limit: costs
$O(U)$ memory for the universe, and only works because prices are bounded and
dense --- unbounded keys put you back in the comparison model. The tree's
$O(\log k)$ is \emph{unconditional}; that generality is what the log buys.

\subsection*{Theory 5: bitmap + bitscan for ``best price''}

The level array is mostly empty --- need ``which prices are live'' without
scanning. One bit per price; finding best ask = \texttt{tzcnt} (count trailing
zeros) --- a single CPU \emph{instruction}, not a loop: all 64 bits exist as
voltages simultaneously; a priority-encoder circuit answers in ~1 cycle.

\begin{diagram}
prices:  4900 4901 4902 4903
bitmap:    0    0    1    1       lowest set bit = best ask
                  ^
one word covers 64 prices; 10k prices = ~1.2KB = permanently L1-resident
bids use lzcnt (highest bit); "next nonempty level" = same instruction
\end{diagram}

Pattern worth naming: \emph{encode the problem as a question the hardware
answers natively} (chess bitboards, OS scheduler runqueues). Worst case is
universe-proportional ($O(U/64)$ word scans) --- fixable with a hierarchical
bitmap (bits over words), the practical cousin of vEB's $O(\log\log U)$.

\subsection*{Theory 6: mutation is where trees bleed}

Lookups were the warmup; a book mostly \emph{writes}. A tree insert =
\texttt{malloc} + descent + rebalance (writes to ancestors you didn't ask
about). And \texttt{malloc} is a global, lock-protected, \textbf{unbounded}
call --- 50ns usually, microseconds when the heap is in a bad state:

\begin{diagram}
naive bench:  p50 = 300ns   p99.9 = 5600ns   max ~2ms
              ^--- that spread IS allocator/rebalance jitter
\end{diagram}

Amortized averages don't matter when the metric is the tail. The flat
version's whole mutation path is a few bounded writes to computable addresses
--- ``bounded tail'' means \emph{there is no code path that can take 2ms}.

\subsection*{Theory 7: the pool, and the pardoned linked list}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pool/arena} for orders: one big array, alloc = bump pointer.
        No \texttt{new}/\texttt{delete} in the hot path; stable addresses mean
        \texttt{OrderId -> Order*} stays valid forever.
  \item \textbf{Intrusive FIFO} per level: orders link to each other; cancel
        = unlink two pointers, $O(1)$, no search. It's a linked list ---
        case-2 memory --- but you only ever touch head/tail, so the chase is
        \emph{bounded at one hop}. The rule isn't ``never chase pointers,''
        it's ``never chase unboundedly.''
  \item \textbf{Identity lookup} (\texttt{cancel(47)}): flat hash map ---
        open addressing = probing contiguous slots (case-1), vs
        \texttt{unordered\_map}'s chained case-2 buckets. If ids are dense,
        direct indexing beats hashing entirely --- the concept-4 escape
        applied a second time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Theory 8: the composite is the real structure}

Two indexes over the same objects (by price, by id) must stay in sync on every
mutation --- a fill that removes an order from its level but leaves it in the
id map creates a phantom. Textbooks give you structures; \emph{keeping indexes
consistent under mutation is the actual engineering.}

\subsection*{Theory: percentiles are the truth, the mean is a summary}

Tail latency is the product: p99.9 exposes allocator slow paths, rehashes,
rebalances. A fair benchmark controls warmup, allocator state, and seed ---
and ``my fast version was slower at X, here's why'' earns more credibility
than any headline number. You are measuring your code \emph{and} your
harness; know which is which.

\subsection*{Theory: what the numbers can claim}

Matching $k$ levels costs $O(k)$ in fills --- inherent work, correctly paid.
What you removed is per-operation overhead and unbounded variance. The
defensible claim: \emph{``I exploited bounded dense integer keys to leave the
comparison model, and contiguous inline layout to leave pointer chasing ---
here's what my design still can't do.''}
